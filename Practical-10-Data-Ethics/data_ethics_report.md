# Practical 10 — Ethical Considerations in Data Privacy

## Objective
Examine ethical questions arising from data collection, analysis, security and technology design.

## 1. Privacy versus security
Security monitoring can protect people and systems, but excessive monitoring can become surveillance. Apply necessity, proportionality, transparency, limited access and retention controls. Prefer targeted controls over indiscriminate collection.

## 2. Impact on marginalized communities
Datasets may under-represent people with disabilities, low-income groups, rural communities or minority-language users. Historical data can encode structural discrimination. A model may produce unequal error rates or deny access to essential services.

**Mitigations:** representative data review, subgroup performance testing where lawful and appropriate, participatory consultation, accessible notices, human review and appeal channels.

## 3. Consent and meaningful choice
A long or confusing notice does not create meaningful understanding. Avoid dark patterns, bundled consent and unnecessary collection. Explain what is collected, why, who receives it and how choices can be changed.

## 4. Purpose limitation and data minimisation
Do not collect data merely because it might be useful later. Define the purpose first, collect the minimum needed and prevent incompatible secondary uses.

## 5. Transparency and explainability
People should receive understandable explanations of consequential automated decisions. Provide a route to question or appeal decisions and involve a human where appropriate.

## 6. Accountability
Assign owners, document design decisions, conduct impact assessments for high-risk processing, test systems before deployment and monitor harms after launch.

## Example ethical risk register

| Scenario | Potential harm | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Face recognition used for attendance | Misidentification and intrusive tracking | Offer less intrusive alternatives; test error rates |
| Loan model trained on biased historical data | Discriminatory outcomes | Data review, fairness testing, human appeal |
| Location data retained indefinitely | Stalking or profiling | Minimise precision and retention; strict access |
| Student analytics shared widely | Stigma and loss of autonomy | Purpose limits, aggregation and access controls |

## Ethical review questions
- Is the purpose legitimate, necessary and proportionate?
- Could the same goal be achieved with less personal data?
- Who benefits and who may be harmed?
- Are groups missing or misrepresented in the data?
- Can affected people understand, challenge and appeal decisions?
- Who is accountable when something goes wrong?

## Conclusion
Responsible data practice is not only about legal compliance. It requires respect for autonomy, fairness, transparency, security, inclusion and accountability throughout the data lifecycle.
